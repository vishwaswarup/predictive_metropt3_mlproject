Predicting failures of a metro air compressor, and what the data would not let us predict

We built a four-stage pipeline on the MetroPT-3 dataset: anomaly detection, failure prediction, remaining useful life (RUL) estimation and a rule-based maintenance scheduler. Each stage was tested by holding out one whole failure at a time. Only the anomaly detector holds up.

Author: Vishwaswarup RathDataset: MetroPT-3 (UCI #791)Period: Windows analysed: Validation: leave-one-failure-out
Summary. The autoencoder raises an alarm in nearly every window of every documented failure, but it gives meaningful advance warning for only one of the four. A classifier and an RUL model trained on the lead-up to failures did no better than chance or than a constant guess. The scheduler is therefore built around how long an anomaly persists, and treats the other two models as unproven.

1 · The problem

Metro trains rely on an Air Production Unit (APU). It compresses air for brakes, doors and suspension. When it fails in service, trains come out of operation. Scheduled maintenance avoids surprises but replaces parts that are still healthy. Condition-based maintenance tries to act only when the machine shows it needs it.

That needs three things from sensor data: to notice that behaviour has changed, to say whether a failure is coming, and to estimate how long is left. We ask whether each of those is achievable from the 15 sensors on the unit, and how much of it can be trusted when judged on failures the model has never seen.

Questions

  • Can an autoencoder trained only on normal behaviour detect failures and how early?
  • Can a classifier predict a failure within the next 24 hours, and which sensors drive it?
  • Can a regressor estimate hours remaining before failure?
  • Can these be combined into maintenance decisions that a planner could act on?

2 · The dataset

MetroPT-3 contains readings from an APU on a Metro do Porto train between 1 February and 1 September 2020. It has 1,516,948 rows and 15 signals: 8 analogue and 7 digital. The unit logs about every 10 seconds, so the sampling rate is 0.1 Hz rather than 1 Hz. There is no label column. Failure times come from maintenance reports published with the dataset.

Signals

Documented failures

Four air-leak failures are documented. All four lead-ups come after the training period, so the autoencoder never sees them.

Data quirks that shaped the design

  • Logging gaps. The compressor idles for long stretches and nothing is logged. Short gaps (up to 30 s) are forward-filled. Longer ones stay missing and no window may cross them.
  • Flat-lined sensor. The TP3 pressure sensor is stuck on 27 May, 23 June and 24 June. Those days are masked everywhere.
  • Undocumented anomaly on 29 March. Kept out of training and reported separately.
  • Drift. Operating behaviour shifts over the months, most visibly in August. This turns out to matter for everything that follows.

Anomaly detection

An LSTM autoencoder learns to reconstruct 10-minute windows of normal operation. It trains on 69,606 windows from 1 Feb to 14 Apr 2020, with 29 March excluded. The cut-off is 72 hours before the first failure, so no failure lead-up is in training. An 80/20 chronological split gives training and validation sets. Training stopped after epochs, and the best epoch was . A window is anomalous when its reconstruction error exceeds the training mean plus three standard deviations ().

Failure classifier and RUL

Each window is summarised by mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum and skew of each sensor, plus the autoencoder error: 76 features. XGBoost predicts whether a failure begins within 24 hours, with SHAP used to explain it. The RUL model predicts hours to failure inside a 72-hour zone, with the target capped at 72 h and falling linearly to zero.

Validation: leave-one-failure-out

Ordinary random splits would put minutes-apart, near-identical windows in both train and test. We instead split the timeline into four blocks, each holding exactly one failure and its lead-up. Each model trains on three blocks and is scored on the fourth. Windows inside a failure are never used for training. The RUL model is always compared with a constant predictor, because a model that learned nothing still looks reasonable on a fixed 0–72 h scale.

Scheduler

Maintenance levels are NORMAL, WATCH, SCHEDULE_MAINTENANCE and URGENT. Likelihood (0–3) rises with how long the anomaly has persisted, and a risk matrix combines it with consequence. Section 5 explains why persistence, rather than anomaly score or model probabilities, is the driver.

5 · Maintenance scheduler

Because the classifier and RUL model have no skill, the scheduler cannot rely on them. It uses the one signal that works, the anomaly flag. A flag counts once five consecutive windows (5 min) exceed the threshold. Then likelihood rises with how long it lasts: possible, then likely after 60 min, almost certain after 240 min. A risk matrix multiplies likelihood by consequence, fixed at major for an air leak.

Classifier probability and RUL can add one likelihood step to an active anomaly, but that option is switched off. When enabled, false SCHEDULE-or-higher episodes rose from 34 to 150 with only modest gains in lead time.

6 · Discussion

Why prediction failed

  • Four events. Leave-one-failure-out leaves three failures to learn from. The failures also differ in length (4.5 h to 52.5 h), so there is little repeated structure.
  • Drift. Operating behaviour changes across months, and a model that keys on absolute levels learns the season, not the failure.
  • Abrupt onset. Air leaks may not give a gradual signature in these sensors. The detector sees the leak once it is happening, not the lead-up to it.

Limitations

  • The false-alarm rate is high: around one in five healthy windows leaves NORMAL, mostly through drift.
  • Thresholds (60 and 240 min, risk cut-offs) were set by looking at these four failures, so the lead-time numbers are optimistic.
  • The 24 h and 72 h horizons are choices, not tuned values. The sweep shows the result is not sensitive to the first.
  • The consequence class is a configured constant, not derived from cost data.

What would help

  • Handle drift directly, for example by periodically refitting the scaler and threshold or detecting regime changes.
  • Compare against the sparse autoencoder and variational autoencoder from the reference paper, and add its low-pass filter on the detector output.
  • Separate analogue-only and digital-only detectors, as the paper does, since it finds air leaks show up in analogue signals.
  • More failure events, or data from other trains.

7 · Conclusion

What works. An LSTM autoencoder trained on pre-failure normal data flags the documented air-leak failures and the undocumented 29 March anomaly. It can drive a maintenance alert once an anomaly persists.
What does not. On this data we could not predict a failure ahead of time, and we could not estimate time to failure. Both fall to chance or below under leave-one-failure-out validation. The scheduler therefore delivers mostly late alerts, with advance notice on one failure out of four.

The useful result is the method as much as the model: validate on whole held-out failures, compare against a constant baseline, and report the negative results. They changed the design of the scheduler, which would otherwise have trusted two models that cannot be trusted.
